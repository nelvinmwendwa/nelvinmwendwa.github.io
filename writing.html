01 · DATA

When public records become a software problem

The Compliance Intelligence Platform started with a fairly ordinary observation: useful public information is often spread across pages that were not designed to work together. The difficult part was not making a search box. It was deciding what counted as a record, how to identify the same person across different sources, and how to keep the original source attached to the data.

That changed the way I think about “clean data”. A clean table is not automatically trustworthy. If a record cannot be traced back to where it came from, cleaning has removed part of the information that matters. The project therefore grew around citations, source records, positions and institutions instead of treating them as metadata to add later.

02 · QUANT

A backtest is evidence, not a promise

Working on the Deriv R_50 system has made me much more suspicious of attractive numbers. A strategy can look interesting over a sample of ticks and still fall apart when the sample changes, the entry timing moves, or the assumptions become more realistic.

That is why the bot has gradually become less about “finding a signal” and more about the machinery around the signal: walk-forward testing, contract tracking, risk limits, logging and knowing exactly what happened after an entry. The useful lesson has not been a particular trading rule. It has been learning to separate a hypothesis from evidence that actually supports it.

03 · BACKEND

The part of automation nobody puts in the screenshot

The interesting moment in an automation project is usually the first successful run. The important work starts after that. APIs time out. HTML changes. A source returns an error page instead of the data you expected. A duplicate record appears. A deployment works locally and behaves differently in production.

Building the compliance and sanctions systems has pushed me toward the less glamorous parts of Python: validation, retries, logging, database constraints, small verification scripts and being able to inspect what the pipeline actually did. Those details are what turn a script that works once into software that can be trusted to run again.

04 · PRODUCT

Building the interface after understanding the problem

AfriTick has been a useful counterweight to my backend-heavy projects. It is easy to get absorbed in implementation and forget that someone still has to understand the product. The marketplace concept forced me to think about hierarchy, trust, presentation and what information a person actually needs before taking the next step.

I am interested in the overlap: systems that are technically sound without becoming difficult for a real person to use.
